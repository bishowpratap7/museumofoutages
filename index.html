The Museum of Outages

A permanent exhibition of the days the internet fell over.

Every exhibit in this museum really happened, cost somebody a fortune, and taught the whole industry something it should already have known. Wander the wings: deployments that ate companies, configurations that erased countries' worth of websites, and one very small room for a very famous typo. Each plaque gives the date, the damage, what actually happened — and the lesson, in one line.

Wing I — The Deployment Wing

Nothing was wrong with the plan. The plan was shipped.

Exhibit No. 1 · Deployment Wing

Knight Capital: The 45-Minute Fortune

Knight Capital Group · August 1, 2012 · New York
≈45 minutesDuration
≈$440 millionLost
1 of 8 serversMissed the update
9.4 / 10Pandey Scale

A technician deployed new trading code to seven of the firm's eight servers. The eighth kept an old flag whose original feature had been retired years earlier — and whose code, when woken, began firing millions of unintended orders into the market. By the time anyone could stop it, Knight had lost about $440 million and, effectively, the company.

The lesson: Dead code is a loaded gun, and a deployment you didn't verify on every machine is a rumor, not a release.

Exhibit No. 2 · Deployment Wing

Cloudflare: One Rule to Slow Them All

Cloudflare · July 2, 2019 · Global
≈30 minutesSevere impact
1 regexRoot cause
Global, instantlyRollout
7.8 / 10Pandey Scale

A single firewall rule containing a regular expression prone to catastrophic backtracking was pushed to Cloudflare's entire global network at once. Edge servers burned all their CPU matching the pattern, and a large share of the web behind Cloudflare stalled until the rule was rolled back.

The lesson: A global, instant rollout means a global, instant blast radius. Canary everything — especially the safety systems.

Exhibit No. 3 · Deployment Wing · The Centerpiece

CrowdStrike: The Day the World Blue-Screened

CrowdStrike / Microsoft Windows · July 19, 2024 · Worldwide
≈8.5 millionWindows machines crashed
DaysFull recovery for many
1 content fileRoot cause
10 / 10Pandey Scale

A routine content update for CrowdStrike's Falcon sensor contained a flaw that crashed the Windows machines it protected — an estimated 8.5 million of them, by Microsoft's count. Airlines grounded flights, hospitals postponed procedures, broadcasters went off air, and because the fix often required hands on each keyboard, recovery took days in many places. It is widely regarded as the largest IT outage in history.

The lesson: A content update is a code update. Anything that ships to every machine at once, at kernel level, deserves a staged rollout and a rehearsed undo.

Wing II — The Hall of Configuration

The systems were up. The map to reach them was not.

Exhibit No. 4 · Hall of Configuration

Fastly: The Customer Who Broke the Web by Using It Correctly

Fastly · June 8, 2021 · Global
49 minutesTo 95% restored
85% of networkReturning errors
27 daysThe bug lay dormant
8.1 / 10Pandey Scale

A software update in May had quietly planted a bug in Fastly's CDN. Four weeks later, one customer pushed a perfectly valid configuration change — and lit the fuse. Within a minute, 85% of Fastly's network was returning errors, taking major news sites, commerce platforms and a government portal with it. Fastly detected it in about a minute and restored 95% of the network within 49 minutes, then published a postmortem so honest it belongs in a frame of its own. Read Fastly's summary.

The lesson: Dormant bugs wait for valid inputs, not attacks. And a fast, honest postmortem can turn your worst hour into your best advertisement.

Exhibit No. 5 · Hall of Configuration

Facebook: The Day the Internet's Biggest House Lost Its Own Address

Meta (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp) · October 4, 2021 · Global
≈6 hoursDuration
3 appsVanished at once
BGP routesWithdrawn by config
9.7 / 10Pandey Scale

During routine maintenance, a command intended to check backbone capacity withdrew the BGP routes that told the world where Facebook's DNS servers lived. Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp didn't crash — they became unfindable, which from the outside is the same thing. Recovery was slowed by a special cruelty: the internal tools, and even building access systems, depended on the same network that had just forgotten how to reach itself.

The lesson: Your rescue tools must never depend on the thing they're rescuing. Out-of-band access is not a luxury; it's the fire exit.

Exhibit No. 6 · Hall of Configuration

AWS: The Automation That Congested Its Own Cloud

Amazon Web Services, US-EAST-1 · December 7, 2021
≈7 hoursDisruption
1 regionThat half the web rents
Internal networkCongested itself
8.6 / 10Pandey Scale

Automated activity meant to scale capacity in AWS's internal network triggered an unexpected flood of connection attempts, congesting the link between that internal network and the main AWS network. Streaming services stuttered, delivery logistics wobbled, and thousands of companies discovered which of their "redundant" systems all lived in the same region in Virginia.

The lesson: Automation fails at the same scale it succeeds. And "multi-AZ in one region" is a plan for weather, not for this.

Wing III — The Typo Room

A small room. One exhibit. Enormous echo.

Exhibit No. 7 · The Typo Room

AWS S3: The Four-Hour Typo

Amazon Web Services, S3 · February 28, 2017
≈4 hoursS3 impaired
1 mistyped commandRoot cause
Far more serversRemoved than intended
8.9 / 10Pandey Scale

While debugging a billing issue, an engineer ran a command with a typo in it. Instead of removing a small set of servers, it removed far more — including the subsystems S3 needed to serve requests and to restart quickly. A remarkable share of the internet's storage went read-only for hours, and the restart itself took longer than AWS's design had ever rehearsed.

The lesson: Guardrails beat careful people: dry runs, blast-radius limits, and restarts you have actually practiced at full scale.

Wing IV — Fire, Power & Malice

Not all outages are made of software.

Exhibit No. 8 · Fire, Power & Malice

Dyn: The Webcam Army

Dyn (DNS) · October 21, 2016 · US & Europe
All day, in wavesDuration
Mirai botnetWeapon
Cameras & DVRsThe soldiers
8.4 / 10Pandey Scale

The Mirai botnet — an army of hijacked cameras, DVRs and other forgotten internet gadgets — flooded DNS provider Dyn with traffic. Because so many major sites relied on that one directory, household names became unreachable in waves across the US East Coast and Europe, even though their own servers were perfectly healthy.

The lesson: DNS is a single point of failure wearing a trench coat. Two providers, or one very bad day.

Exhibit No. 9 · Fire, Power & Malice

British Airways: The Power Cable Incident

British Airways · May 27, 2017 · London Heathrow & Gatwick
A bank-holiday weekendDuration
≈75,000Passengers affected
Power supplyRoot cause
8.8 / 10Pandey Scale

A power failure at a data center — the airline blamed a power supply issue, with reports of an accidental disconnection and a damaging surge on restoration — toppled BA's systems worldwide at the start of a holiday weekend. Hundreds of flights were cancelled, tens of thousands of passengers slept in terminals, and the recovery cost the airline tens of millions of pounds.

The lesson: The most powerful admin interface in any data center is still a power cable. Protect it like production, because it is.

Exhibit No. 10 · Fire, Power & Malice

OVHcloud: The Night the Cloud Caught Fire

OVHcloud, Strasbourg · March 10, 2021 · France
1 data centerDestroyed
Thousands of sitesOffline
Same-building backupsLost with the originals
9.1 / 10Pandey Scale

Fire tore through OVHcloud's SBG2 data center in Strasbourg in the middle of the night, destroying it and damaging its neighbor. No one was hurt — the one perfect outcome — but thousands of websites went dark, and customers whose backups lived in the same facility discovered that their copies had burned beside the originals.

The lesson: A backup that shares a fate with the original is not a backup. It's a second copy of the same loss.

Wing V — The Partition Gallery

Forty-three seconds of silence; a day of consequences.

Exhibit No. 11 · The Partition Gallery

GitHub: Forty-Three Seconds, Twenty-Four Hours

GitHub · October 21–22, 2018 · Global
43 secondsNetwork partition
24 h 11 minDegraded service
0Data lost
9.0 / 10Pandey Scale

During maintenance on optical equipment, GitHub's East and West Coast facilities lost sight of each other for 43 seconds. Automated failover promoted West Coast database primaries — but a few seconds of East Coast writes hadn't replicated, and when the link healed, the two sides disagreed about the truth. GitHub chose to protect the data rather than the uptime, and spent a day reconciling by hand. The final count: zero data lost, and a masterclass in choosing the right thing to sacrifice. Read GitHub's analysis.

The lesson: Consistency versus availability isn't a textbook diagram; it's a decision you'll make at 3 a.m. Make it in advance, in writing.

About this museum

The Museum of Outages is curated by Bishow Pandey, a senior software and platform engineer who works on Kubernetes, cloud infrastructure and application security — which is to say, a man professionally invested in keeping new exhibits out of this building. Every exhibit is summarized in the museum's own words from the company's public postmortem or official statement; figures are as reported at the time. The Pandey Scale (0–10) is the curator's entirely subjective rating of an outage's scale, story and teaching value.

The collection grows when an incident is big enough to make the news, strange enough to teach something new, and honest enough to publish a postmortem. The Emergency Room above watches the major status pages, looking for future exhibits.

A note on accuracy: outage history is told by the companies involved, and sometimes retold loosely. This museum sticks to the official postmortems and contemporaneous reporting, and marks estimates with ≈. If you spot an error on a plaque, tell the curator on X — corrections are made on the record.
